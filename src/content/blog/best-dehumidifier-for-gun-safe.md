## Rod or desiccant – which gun safe dehumidifier fits your safe

**A gun safe dehumidifier comes in two kinds: heated rods that keep the air warmer than the steel so moisture never condenses, and desiccants that soak up water until they are saturated.** A safe in a dry, heated house usually needs one. A humid garage or basement often needs both.

### What a heated rod does and does not do

The heated rod warms the air slightly. Warm air rises, cooler air sinks past the rod, and the whole safe stays a little above the dew point, the temperature at which water vapor turns to liquid on a cold surface. That stops condensation on barrels and actions. What it does not do is remove moisture, which a commenter on a collectors' forum thread put plainly: "the majority of the in-safe electronic dehumidifiers are just electric heaters with the aim being to prevent condensation." That is accurate, and it is fine as long as you plan for it. In a very humid unconditioned garage, owners report a rod alone was not enough.

### When silica or a rechargeable canister is the better call

A silica gel desiccant is the better call in these situations:

- There is no outlet near the safe, or no way to pass a cord.
- The safe is small, like a bedside box or a document box.
- You need a second layer next to a rod in a humid garage or basement.
- The safe is fire-rated. Forum posters say the fire-rated body can hold moisture in its walls and seals, so humidity control matters more. If you are shopping for one, our [fireproof safe guide](/blog/best-fireproof-safe/) covers how those ratings are certified.

## How to size a safe dehumidifier to your safe

**Size a safe dehumidifier by the interior volume of your safe in cubic feet, then pick the rod length or the number of canisters that covers it.** Most home gun safes come out far below the 100 cubic feet that Lockdown rates its shortest rods for.

### Match rod length to cubic feet

Follow these steps to get the number you need:

1. Measure the interior height, width and depth in inches.
2. Multiply the three and divide by 1,728 to get cubic feet.
3. Match rods: about 100 cu ft for a 12 inch Lockdown rod and about 200 cu ft for the 18 inch, per the maker.
4. Match silica: about 50 cu ft per 750 g can, per Gun University and American Firearms.

As an example, an interior of 55 by 24 by 18 inches works out to about 13.8 cubic feet. That is well inside what one 12 inch rod or one 750 g can is rated for, so for most of us placement and climate matter more than length. Longer rods make sense for very large safes and vault rooms.

### Running the cord out of a closed safe

The rods need a 110-120 V outlet and a way for the cord to reach it. Some safes come with a factory cord pass-through, so check your manual first. Lockdown lists a quick-plug attachment for the Golden Rod, and a SIG Talk owner says it detaches to feed through a small hole. Never pinch the cord in the door, since the door then cannot close fully. If there is no pass-through, ask your safe maker before drilling, especially in a fire-rated safe, because a new hole in the body is a change the maker should sign off on.

### Where to place it and what to keep off it

Placement decides whether the warm air actually moves. Keep these points in mind:

- Mount a rod horizontally at the lowest point of the safe, on its brackets or legs.
- Keep polymer stocks, holsters and ammunition from touching a rod, which owners say gets hot.
- Put a silica can on a shelf where air reaches it, not buried behind cases.
- Leave gaps between guns so air can circulate.
- Keep the safe bolted down as its maker directs; a dehumidifier does not change that. Our [safe buying checklist](/safe-buying-checklist/) covers anchoring.

Lockdown rates its 12 inch rods for about 100 cubic feet, yet the silica canister's page gives no coverage at all, so the 50 cu ft figure comes from those two guides. Then we did the math on a sample long-gun safe interior and got under 14 cubic feet. The forum threads told the rest of the story: the rust problems we read about were mostly cold walls, garage slabs and gun socks, not the wrong rod length.

## Problems owners run into with a gun safe dehumidifier, and the fix

**Most gun safe dehumidifier complaints trace back to four problems: rust anyway, a sweating safe on a cold wall or slab, units that die, and silica that saturates within weeks.** Each one has a practical fix.

### Rust even with a dehumidifier running

Guns that rust inside the safe even with silica packs and a dehumidifier is a recurring complaint on The High Road and AR15.com. A common culprit is fabric. In one High Road rust thread, a member advised: "Get rid of the gun socks, they attract moisture and don't allow for air to move evaporating moisture." Gun socks and foam barrel rests hold moisture against steel, and saturated silica packs do the same. The fix:

- Pull gun socks and felt or foam rests, or let them dry out first.
- Wipe a light coat of oil on bare steel, since no device replaces oil on steel in a humid climate.
- Put a hygrometer inside and run a rod plus desiccant until it reads steady.

If you find light specks, the original poster of an r/Firearms thread about a safe left closed for weeks added in an edit: "it's just surface rust. Should just wipe off." Anything deeper is a job for a gunsmith.

### Cold walls, slabs and garage condensation

A safe against an exterior wall or on a garage slab that sweats is the second recurring complaint. In an [AR15.com humidity thread](https://ar15.com/forums/general/humidity-problems-in-the-gunsafe--help-needed/5-2079886/), one reply diagnosed it: "Sounds like the outside wall is chilling the safe and causing condensation, like dew on a toilet bowl." In a High Road garage thread, an owner wrote: "I do not want to set it directly on the concrete as the concrete sweats and will cause rust." The fix is to move the safe off the exterior wall if you can, leave an air gap behind it, and keep it off bare concrete on a pad or pedestal that still lets you anchor it the way the maker intends. A heated rod then keeps the steel and air inside above the dew point.

### Units that fail, freeze or only heat

Dehumidifiers that die, freeze up or turn out to be heaters are the third pattern. A High Road member said the dehumidifier he had been using quit earlier in the year, and an owner in the garage thread above found a stronger room dehumidifier froze up despite a defrost feature. An in-safe rod has no coils to freeze, and the ones owners describe running for 15 years or more are simple heated rods. The Golden Rod carries a lifetime warranty, per Lockdown, which is the backstop for the single report we found of one quitting after a year. Touch the rod housing near the plug now and then, or watch the hygrometer, to confirm it is still working.

### Silica that changes color within a week or two

Silica that saturates within weeks is the fourth complaint, and one owner in The High Road rust thread says his packs changed color within a week or two every time. The collectors' forum thread above warns that desiccant needs frequent recharging and only does its full job in an airtight space. In a humid garage, a desiccant that is too small for the load ends up saturated fast. The fix is a bigger 750 g can instead of small packs, a rod to cut the load, and a recharge schedule you actually keep.

## Keeping gun safe humidity control on track long term

**Gun safe humidity control is a habit more than a gadget: a hygrometer, a recharge reminder and a light coat of oil do more than any single device.** We would check all three monthly.

### Hygrometer target and checks

A hygrometer is a small gauge that reads relative humidity, and it is the only way to know your setup works. Liberty Safe puts the ideal range at 40 to 50 percent, and Gun University's reviewer finds 45 to 55 percent a good zone. Check it weekly for the first month, then after long humid spells or after the door has stood open. If the reading climbs, recharge the silica, confirm the rod is warm and look at the wall and floor behind the safe.

### Recharging desiccant

When the beads change color, take the can out and bake it per the included instructions. Let it cool fully before you handle it, since owners say the hot can is the worst part of the job. Set a phone reminder so it does not sit saturated for weeks. Storage rules also still apply: a dehumidifier does not change your state and local firearm storage laws, so check your state's official firearm storage page for what your home requires.
